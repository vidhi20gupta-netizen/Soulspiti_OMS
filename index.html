Context

A craft business hitting an invisible ceiling

SoulSpiti makes handcrafted wood furniture — sheesham coffee tables, walnut dining sets — coordinated through a network of independent artisans in Rajasthan. In January 2024, the business was running on founder Avantika's WhatsApp messages, Google Sheets, and intuition. It was working. Until it wasn't.

At 100 orders/month, the system began to fracture. Not gradually — violently.

The breaking point · Valentine's Day 2024
Avantika sent 8 orders via WhatsApp to Ramesh: "8 tables for Valentine's — can you do?" He replied हाँ. He thought: 1 order, 8 tables. She meant: 8 separate customer orders. He made 2 tables and considered himself done. Six customers received nothing. ₹86,000 lost. Viral complaints followed.

Root cause wasn't Ramesh. It was a system built for one that was being asked to serve thousands.

This is the moment the project started — not from a product roadmap or OKR, but from a real failure with real financial and reputational cost.

Attempt 1 · Feb 2024

The automation that taught me to do research first

The obvious fix was automation. I hired an Upwork developer (₹40K) and built a Twilio WhatsApp bot. It sent orders in English, expected clean replies like "Reply YES", and had no timeout logic. It went live. And it immediately failed.

Failure analysis · 68% non-response rate
Artisans preferred Hindi — they didn't engage with English messages. The rigid reply format rejected हाँ, ok, okay. Messages arrived at random hours; artisans only checked their phones during chai breaks (10 AM, 3 PM, 7 PM). No timeout meant missed messages sat unanswered indefinitely.

Abandoned after 2 weeks. ₹40K wasted. But the real cost was clarity: I had automated a process I didn't understand.
User Research · April 2024

Six hours in a workshop in Jodhpur

I flew to Jodhpur and sat with Ramesh, Suresh, and Dinesh in their workshops — 6+ hours each. No surveys. No forms. Just watching how they actually worked.

"Meet artisans where they are, not where we wish they were."

Design principle, Jodhpur trip
☕
Chai break pattern
Artisans check WhatsApp exactly 3 times/day — 10 AM, 3 PM, 7 PM during breaks. Messages outside those windows go unseen for hours.
💰
Money-first reading
First scan: how much do I get paid? If payment isn't visible in the first line, they read for context before deciding. Payment in line 1 = faster decisions.
✂️
2-line limit
Nobody reads more than 2 lines. Anything longer was skipped or handed to someone else. The message has to work in a glance.
🇮🇳
Hindi primary
English messages felt formal and foreign. Hindi was natural. Every acceptance variation — हाँ, ok, okay, haan — needed to be treated as yes.
📦
Capacity ceiling
Max 10–12 orders/month per artisan. Beyond this, quality dropped. Any routing logic had to hardcode this ceiling.
🪵
Weekly restocks
Wood inventory refreshed weekly. Routing had to check material availability, not just artisan availability. These are different states.
Solution V2 · Apr–Jun 2024

Rebuilding the message from scratch

The new template wasn't just translated to Hindi. It was restructured around how artisans actually read and decide. Payment first. Two lines max. Flexible reply acceptance.

WhatsApp order template · V2
🪑 नया आर्डर - Sheesham Coffee Table ₹5,500 मिलेंगे: • अभी: ₹1,650 (30%) • डिलीवरी पर: ₹3,850 (70%) Deadline: 15 May हाँ/ना में reply करें
Delivered 10:02 AM
Timeout logic
Wait 6 hrs → reminder → wait 4 hrs → route to next artisan + alert founder. No more silent black holes.
Flexible parsing
Accepted: हाँ, yes, ok, okay, haan, ha, y. Rejected = ना, no, nahi, busy. Any other text → flags to Avantika.

OMS V2.0 · Jan–Mar 2025

The system that runs itself

In January 2025, I spent 120 hours across 3 weeks learning Make.com and Airtable from scratch — YouTube tutorials, practice automations, integration testing. The decision to go no-code wasn't passive; it was a deliberate trade-off.

Build vs buy decision
Custom dev: ₹3–5L one-time + ₹25K/month, 4+ months, external dependency.
No-code: ₹7K/month, 8 weeks, Vidhi owns it entirely.

Chose no-code. Speed, cost, and ownership all pointed the same direction.

Photo QC · Jun 2025

Catching issues before they ship

With routing automated, the next constraint was quality. At 16–17% complaint rate, defects were being discovered after delivery — unreturnable, unrepairable, already viral on Instagram. I needed a quality gate before the order left the workshop.

1
Artisan marks "Packed" in Airtable
Status change triggers the QC workflow automatically
2
Auto-WhatsApp: "Send 3 photos"
Front view, back view, packaging — specific angles reduce ambiguity
3
Photos → Cloudinary → Slack alert
Avantika or Vidhi receive a notification with images linked inline
4
Review within 6 hours
Approve → ship. Reject → artisan fixes + resubmits. No shipping without approval.
Results · Jun 2025
Compliance: 92% · Issues caught pre-dispatch: 13–15% · Complaint rate: 16% → 12.5%

What I learned

The non-obvious lessons

Research before automation, always
The first bot failed because I automated my assumptions. The Jodhpur trip cost 3 days and changed everything. There is no substitute for watching users work.
94% is the right target
Chasing 100% auto-routing would have made the system brittle. The 6% human override handles edge cases that no rule can anticipate. Hybrid > perfect automation.
Ownership beats customisation
A system I can debug at 11 PM on a Saturday is worth more than a more powerful one I depend on a developer to touch. No-code was the right call.
Progress bars reduce complaints
Customers don't complain about slow — they complain about silent. The daily 6 PM update dropped complaints 60%, without changing actual delivery speed at all.
Dashboards > alerts
Notifications create anxiety. Real-time dashboards create control. Avantika's role shifted from firefighter to decision-maker because she could see the state of every order at any time.
Roll out to 3 before 30
Progressive rollout: 3 artisans → 7 → 14 → 30. Each stage revealed failure modes that would have been catastrophic at full scale. Slowness was the safety feature.
What I'd validate next

The questions still open

The system is stable. These are the questions that would drive the next phase of work.

1. Can routing quality improve further? The 6% failure rate clusters in specific order types — high-complexity pieces and non-standard wood combinations. A more granular skill taxonomy might push routing above 96%.

2. What's the complaint composition at 12.5%? We know the rate but not the breakdown. A 2-week tagging exercise on incoming complaints would tell us whether photo QC, routing, or artisan skill is the remaining constraint.

3. Can artisans self-report inventory? Currently, inventory is manually updated weekly. If artisans could update stock directly via WhatsApp reply, the data would be more accurate and the workflow lighter.

4. What breaks at 600 orders/month? The system was designed for the current load. Before pushing growth, I'd stress-test the Make.com scenarios for high-concurrency edge cases.
